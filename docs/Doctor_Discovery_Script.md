# Doctor Discovery Script

**For the first 20 conversations. Not a sales call.**

---

## Rules before you walk in

1. **Do not open your laptop for the first 15 minutes.** The moment you demo, the doctor starts reacting to your product and stops describing their day. You cannot get that information back.
2. **Never ask "would you use this?" or "would you pay for this?"** People are kind, especially to students. They will say yes and mean nothing by it. Ask about the past, not the future — what someone *did* last Tuesday is evidence; what they *would* do is a wish.
3. **Ask for two introductions at the end. Every time.** This is how you get twenty meetings.
4. **Write notes immediately after, not during.** Taking notes mid-answer changes what people tell you. Sit outside for five minutes and write it down while it's fresh.
5. **Target: 20 minutes.** Ask for ten, and stop at twenty unless they keep talking.

---

## Opening (30 seconds)

> "Doctor, I'm Ishaan — I'm a student, and I'm trying to build something for clinics like yours. But I've realised I don't actually understand how your day works, and I don't want to build the wrong thing. Could I ask you a few questions about how you run your practice? I'm not selling anything today."

If they ask what you're building, say: *"I'll show you at the end if there's time — I'd rather hear about your side first, otherwise I'll just end up asking leading questions."* Most doctors respect this.

---

## Section 1 — The shape of the day

*Goal: understand the actual workload before introducing any concept.*

1. How many patients did you see yesterday?
2. Walk me through yesterday from when you arrived to when you left. What did the day look like?
3. What time did the last patient leave, and what time did you leave?
4. → *If there's a gap:* What were you doing in between?
5. What's the part of the day you most wish you could hand to someone else?

**Listen for:** does anything about records, writing, or files come up *without you raising it*? Mark this. It is the single most important observation of the whole conversation.

---

## Section 2 — Records, without leading

*Goal: find out how history actually gets retrieved. Still don't mention software.*

6. When a patient you saw six months ago walks back in, how do you find out what you gave them last time?
7. → What happens when they don't bring the file?
8. → How often does that happen?
9. What do you write down during a consultation, and what do you skip?
10. Who else in the clinic touches the records — an assistant, a compounder, the pharmacy?
11. Has a patient ever come back with a complication you'd have caught if you'd had their earlier record in front of you?

**Listen for:** whether missing history is an irritation or an actual clinical risk in their eyes. These are very different problems and only one of them is urgent.

---

## Section 3 — What they've tried

*Goal: this is the section that predicts your own failure. Take it seriously.*

12. Have you ever used any software in the clinic — for records, appointments, billing, anything?
13. → *If yes:* What was it, and are you still using it?
14. → *If they stopped:* What made you stop? (Ask this slowly. Let them talk. Do not defend software.)
15. → *If never:* Has anyone tried to sell you clinic software? What did you think?
16. What do you pay for, monthly, to run this clinic? (Software, services, subscriptions of any kind.)

**Listen for:** the reason they abandoned the last system. Whatever it is — too slow, staff wouldn't use it, internet, the salesperson vanished — it will happen to you too unless you design against it specifically.

---

## Section 4 — The patient side

*Goal: test the alternative wedge. If documentation isn't the pain, this might be.*

17. Of the patients you see, how many come back for follow-up when you ask them to?
18. → What do you do when they don't?
19. Do you have patients with diabetes or blood pressure who you'd like to keep track of between visits? How does that work now?
20. What does a patient walk out of here holding?
21. Has a patient or a pharmacy ever come back confused about what you'd written?

**Listen for:** whether a printed, branded prescription and follow-up recall matter more to them than time saved. If several doctors light up here and stayed flat in Section 1, **your wedge is the patient-facing output, not the time saving**, and that is a very cheap pivot to make in week 4.

---

## Section 5 — Language

*Goal: validate the one thing global competitors can't easily do.*

22. What language do you and your patients actually speak during a consultation?
23. What language do you write your notes in?
24. → *If those differ:* Is that ever a problem?

---

## Only now: the demo (5 minutes, optional)

If there's time and interest:

> "Can I show you the thing I've built? It takes two minutes. I mostly want to know what's wrong with it."

Frame it as asking for criticism, not approval. Then:

25. What's the first thing you'd change?
26. What would stop you from using this on Monday morning?
27. What would your staff say about it?

**Do not ask if they'd pay.** If they raise money themselves, that is real signal — note the exact words.

---

## Closing (always)

> "This was really useful, thank you. Two things — could I come back once I've fixed some of what you've told me? And is there a colleague or two you think I should be talking to?"

Get the names. Ask if you can use their name in the introduction. Then actually follow up within a week, even if only to say what you changed.

## Scoring the twenty

At week 4, count:

- **Documentation named unprompted as a top-3 frustration:** ___ / 20
  - **6 or more** → wedge confirmed, proceed to pilots.
  - **Fewer than 6** → do not proceed as planned. Read your notes for what they *did* name. The most likely alternative wedge is patient retention and the printed prescription.
- **Have abandoned clinic software before:** ___ / 20 — and the most common stated reason: ________
- **Willing to be a pilot clinic for free:** ___ / 20 — you need three.
- **Potential clinical advisor identified:** Y / N — you need one, and it is almost certainly one of these twenty.
